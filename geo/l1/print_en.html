3. Video 1 · What shape is the Earth?

Key points
  • The ancient Greeks used observation to show the Earth is round: in a lunar eclipse Earth’s shadow always has a curved edge; traveling south you see stars hidden in the north; a ship sailing away disappears hull first.
  • Zhang Heng of the Eastern Han said the heavens are “like a hen’s egg”: the sky like the shell, the earth like the yolk.

Observe and try (in class): a flashlight, a ball and a plate

Shine a flashlight on a ball, then on a round plate (face-on and edge-on), and look at the shadow on the wall.

Ball’s shadow: ______________________　　Plate’s shadow: ______________________

Think: if the Earth were a flat disk, would we sometimes see a thin line-shaped shadow during a lunar eclipse?

4. Video 2 · How big is the Earth?

Key points
  • Around 240 BC Eratosthenes compared noon shadows in Syene and Alexandria on the summer solstice: the angle was about 7.2°, 1/50 of a circle; distance between the cities × 50 ≈ Earth’s circumference.
  • Today’s measurements: Earth’s average radius is about 6,371 km; the equator is about 40,000 km around. Magellan’s fleet made the first voyage around the world in 1519–1522.

Observe and try (after class, sunny noon): measure a shadow at noon

Stand a chopstick straight up on paper; measure its height and its shadow.Never look straight at the sun; have a parent come along if you go outside.

City: ____________　Date/time: ____________　Height: ______ cm　Shadow: ______ cm　Shadow ÷ height = ______

Think: at noon on the same day, whose shadow is shorter, a student in Jakarta or one in Beijing? Why?

5. Video 3 · Flattening the ball

Key points
  • A sphere can’t be flattened without tearing or stretching, so every flat world map is distorted.
  • The Mercator map (1569) keeps direction but enlarges high-latitude areas: Greenland looks about as big as Africa, but Africa is really about 14 times larger. An equal-area map keeps area and gives up shape.
  • The purpose decides the trade-off. A good map has at least: direction, scale, legend.

Observe and try (after class): an orange-peel world map

Draw an “equator” and a continent on an orange, peel it in one piece, flatten it, and see how the continent changed.

Where it split: ______________________　How the continent changed: ______________________

Think: if you weren’t allowed to tear the peel, what could you do?

6. Think and write

a. Retell: explain to someone who hasn’t taken this lesson how Eratosthenes measured the size of the Earth. (Mention the well, the shadow, the angle and the distance between the cities.)

Sample: at noon on the solstice, sunlight reached the bottom of a well in Syene (sun overhead); at the same time a pole in Alexandria cast a shadow at about 7.2° = 1/50 of a circle; so the distance = 1/50 of the circumference; 5,000 stadia × 50 ≈ 250,000 stadia.

b. Evaluate: a classmate says, “The Mercator map makes Greenland so huge. It’s a wrong map and should be thrown away.” Do you agree? Answer in terms of purpose.

Sample: every flat world map is distorted, so ask what it is good for. The Mercator map keeps direction and suits sailing; it enlarges high-latitude areas, so to compare areas use an equal-area map.

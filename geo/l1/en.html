Observe and try · In class · About 3 min

A flashlight, a ball and a plate

Materials　A phone flashlight, a ball (an orange or apple works too), a round plate or a paper circle, and a white wall or sheet of paper.

  1. Dim the lights. Shine the flashlight on the ball so its shadow falls on the wall or paper.
  2. Shine it from different directions: the front, the side, from above at an angle. Each time, look at the shape of the shadow.
  3. Now use the plate: first shine straight at its face, then from its edge.
Think: if the Earth were a flat disk, would we sometimes see a thin line-shaped shadow during a lunar eclipse?
You may have found:The ball’s shadow is round from every direction. The plate’s shadow is round face-on, but edge-on it becomes a flat oval or even a line. For thousands of years of lunar eclipses, the edge of Earth’s shadow has always been a curve, so the Earth is a ball, not a disk.

Observe and try · After class · Sunny noon · About 10 min

Be Eratosthenes: measure a shadow at noon

Materials　A straight chopstick or pencil, a bit of modeling clay or tape, a sheet of white paper, and a ruler.

  1. On a sunny day around 12 noon, lay the paper flat on level ground in the sun (a windowsill, balcony or playground).
  2. Use the clay to stand the chopstick straight up on the paper. Mark the tip of its shadow with a pen.
  3. Measure the height of the chopstick above the paper, then the length of the shadow. Note the city, date and time.

⚠ Look only at the shadow. Never look straight at the sun. If you go outside your building, have a parent come along; at school, do it with classmates on the playground.

Fill in both numbers and this will work out “shadow ÷ height”.
Think: at noon on the same day, whose shadow is shorter, a student near the equator (say Jakarta) or one in Beijing? Why? If you have friends or relatives in another city, measure at noon on the same day and compare.
Sample answer:The closer to the equator, the higher the noon sun and the shorter the shadow; in Jakarta, on some days the noon shadow almost disappears. When two cities measure at noon on the same day, the difference in their shadows comes from the curve of the ground. That is exactly Eratosthenes’ method. Add the distance between the cities, and you can estimate the size of the Earth.

Observe and try · After class · About 10 min

An orange-peel world map

Materials　An orange and a marker (or ballpoint pen).

  1. Draw an “equator” all the way around the orange, then a big “continent” that crosses the equator and reaches up near the “top” of the orange.
  2. Peel it carefully, keeping the peel in one piece if you can.
  3. Press the peel flat on the table. What happened to your continent? You can take a photo.
Think: if you weren’t allowed to tear the peel, what could you do? Do mapmakers face the same problem?
Sample answer:When you flatten it, the farther from your “equator”, the more cracks. If you can’t tear it, you can only stretch or squeeze it, and that is map “distortion”. Mapmakers face the very same problem, so they either cut the map into segments (like the peel) or accept that some places get stretched or squashed.When you’re done, you can eat the orange.
06

Think · Retell

About 5 min
1. Retell In class
In your own words, explain to a younger brother or sister who hasn’t taken this lesson: how did Eratosthenes measure the size of the Earth?
Mention at least these four things: the well, the shadow, the angle, and the distance between the two cities.
Key points:① At noon on the summer solstice, sunlight in Syene shone straight down a well, so the sun was directly overhead. ② At the same moment a pole in Alexandria cast a shadow; the angle was about 7.2°, roughly 1/50 of a full circle (360°). ③ So the distance between the cities was about 1/50 of Earth’s circumference. ④ The cities were about 5,000 stadia apart; times 50 gives a circumference of about 250,000 stadia.
Check: did you explain clearly why the fraction of the angle equals the fraction of the distance?

Think · Evaluate

In or after class
2. Evaluate In or after class
A classmate says: “The Mercator map makes Greenland so huge. It’s a wrong map and should be thrown away.” Do you agree?
Answer by asking “what was this map drawn for?”, and say what it keeps and what it gives up.
Key points:Every flat world map is distorted, so we can’t just say “right” or “wrong”; we ask “what is it good for?” The Mercator map keeps direction (a straight line keeps a steady heading), so it suits sailing; it gives up area, enlarging places near the poles. To compare countries’ areas, switch to an equal-area map.
A good answer names both the use and the limits, not just one side.

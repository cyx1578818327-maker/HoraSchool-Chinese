Finding Places with the Grid

Textbook page · Key points　Key sentence: latitude first, then longitude, and after reading, check.
  • Reading a point: look at N/S → count lines of latitude from the equator → look at E/W → count lines of longitude from the prime meridian; between two lines, estimate. Writing: 6°S, 107°E = −6.2, 106.8 on a phone; 60 minutes (′) = 1 degree.
  • Four mistakes: latitude and longitude swapped (latitude is never above 90°), north and south mixed up, east and west mixed up, missing numbers (treated as 0°, 0°, “Null Island”).
  • Three ways to check: letters, neighbors, another tool.

1. Opening question (write now, answer again at the end)

A friend sends you the numbers “−8.4, 115.2” with no place name. Without a map app, can you find it on a globe? And once you find it, how do you know you have not got it wrong?

3. Video 1 · Reading a point

Key points
  • Latitude first: look at N/S and count from the equator, then look at E/W and count from the prime meridian; where the two lines cross is the point (30°N, 31°E is Cairo).
  • Between two lines, estimate: 6°S is between the equator and 10°S, a little closer to 10°S.
  • Writing: 6°S, 107°E; a map app writes −6.2, 106.8, where a minus sign means south or west.

A distress telegram (paper version)

In the early hours of 15 April 1912, the Titanic sent a telegram: “Position 41°46′N, 50°14′W”. (60 minutes = 1 degree)

41°46′N is about ______°N, and 50°14′W is about ______°W. about 41.8°N, about 50.2°W

The wreck found on the sea floor in 1985 is at 41°44′N, 49°57′W. In which direction is it from the telegram position? ______ east (slightly south), about 24 km

4. Video 2 · Four common mistakes

Key points
  • Latitude and longitude swapped: latitude only goes up to 90°, so “latitude 107°” must be wrong.
  • North and south mixed up: 6°S written as 6°N is more than 1,300 km off. East and west mixed up: 107°E written as 107°W lands in the eastern Pacific.
  • Missing numbers: a record with no position is often put at 0°, 0° by computers (“Null Island” in the Gulf of Guinea).
  • After writing, ask three questions: Above 90°? North or south right? East or west right?

5. Video 3 · Checking your answer

Key points
  • Letters: N/S and E/W give the big block (N and E is Europe, Asia and northern Africa).
  • Neighbors: compare with a place you know (1°N, 104°E is northwest of Jakarta: Singapore).
  • Another tool: find it again on a globe, a flat map or a map app; only when both land in the same place is it right.

6. Think and write

1. Retell: given “6°S, 107°E”, how do you find it on a globe step by step? How do you check it once you have found it?

Sample: look at S and count south from the equator to 6° (between 0° and 10°S, a little closer to 10°S); then look at E and count east from the prime meridian to 107°. The lines cross at Jakarta. Check: S and E put it in the south and east, among the islands of Southeast Asia; it is not far southeast of Singapore; find it again on a flat map.

2. Evaluate: a classmate says, “A phone finds any place with one tap, so learning to read the grid is useless.” Do you agree?

Sample: phones can be wrong too (the driver on the wrong side of the road, “Null Island”); with no signal or battery they cannot help; in books, old maps or when someone tells you a position you often have only numbers; reading the grid lets you check.

8. Wrap-up: answer the opening question again

Where is “−8.4, 115.2”? How do you know you have not got it wrong? (Use: latitude first, estimate, check.)

Answer: 8.4°S, 115.2°E, the island of Bali, Indonesia, about 1,000 km east of Jakarta.

After class: choose three places (your school, a big city, a place you would like to learn about). Give a family member only the latitude and longitude, ask them to find each on a globe, and help them check with the three ways. Do not write down where your home is.

Video 1 · Observe and try

Observe and try · In class · About 4 min

A rubber-band globe

Materials　An orange or small ball, and three rubber bands (or pieces of string).

  1. Put one rubber band around the “fattest” part of the ball. This is the equator.
  2. Put another on the top half and one on the bottom half. These are two parallels.
  3. Slowly push the top one toward the “top” and watch how its loop changes.
Think: why do parallels get shorter toward the poles? What happens at a pole?
Sample answer:The equator band is longest. A ball gets “thinner” toward its ends, so parallels get shorter toward the poles and shrink to a point at each pole. That’s why latitude only goes up to 90°.

Video 2 · Observe and try

Observe and try · After class · About 10 min

Which square is my city in?

  1. Open a map app, long-press your school or your city center, and read the pair of numbers.
  2. The first number is usually latitude, the second longitude; negative means south or west.
  3. Then find a city you’d like to visit and whose people you want to pray for, and read its coordinates too.

⚠ Only look up your school or city center. Don’t record where your home is.

Think: which of the two cities is closer to the equator? Which is farther east of the prime meridian?
Hint:Compare the latitude numbers (ignore N/S): smaller means closer to the equator. Compare east longitudes: bigger means farther east of the prime meridian. West longitudes count separately.

Video 3 · Observe and try

Observe and try · After class · About 20 min · Evidence for the unit project

Finish my globe

Materials　An old ball, a paper ball or an orange; a few pens of different colors; small labels.

  1. Mark the North and South Poles and draw the equator.
  2. In another color draw the two tropics (about 23.5°), then in another color the two polar circles (about 66.5°).
  3. Draw the prime meridian from the North Pole to the South Pole and write 0°.
  4. Add two labels: my city and a city I want to visit (place them roughly by the coordinates you found earlier). Take a photo.
Check yourself:

The teacher only checks that the lines and places are right, not how neat it looks. Bring it next lesson.

06

Think · Retell

About 5 min
1. Retell In class
Using the movie-theater seats, explain to someone who hasn’t taken this lesson: how does the latitude–longitude grid help us say where a place is?
Mention at least: parallels, meridians, the two starting lines (the equator and the prime meridian), and two numbers.
Key points:A theater uses two numbers, row and seat, to find a seat; the Earth uses two numbers, latitude and longitude, to find a place. Parallels are circles parallel to the equator, counted from 0° at the equator up to 90° north and south; meridians are half-circles joining the poles, counted from 0° at the prime meridian up to 180° east and west. Where two lines cross is the place.
06

Think · Evaluate

In or after class
2. Evaluate
A classmate says: “The prime meridian divides the Eastern and Western Hemispheres.” Is he right?
Say which two meridians geographers usually use, and why.
Key points:Not quite. The prime meridian is where longitude starts, but geographers usually divide the hemispheres with the circle made by 20°W and 160°E. The prime meridian would cut through Britain, France and some West African countries, while 20°W and 160°E run mostly over the ocean, putting Europe and Africa in the Eastern Hemisphere and the Americas in the Western.

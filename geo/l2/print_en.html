3. Video 1 · Drawing a grid on the Earth (parallels and latitude)

Key points
  • The equator is Earth’s “fattest” circle, the 0° parallel. Parallels are circles parallel to the equator; they get shorter toward the poles and shrink to a point at each pole.
  • Latitude is counted from the equator up to 90° north (N) and 90° south (S). Jakarta is about 6°S, Beijing about 40°N.
  • Grid lines are drawn by people on globes and maps; you can’t see them on the ground.
  • In the Northern Hemisphere, Polaris’s height above the horizon is about equal to your latitude; in the Southern Hemisphere you can’t see Polaris.

Observe and try (in class): a rubber-band globe

Put three rubber bands on an orange or ball: one round the “fattest” part (the equator) and one above and one below (parallels). Push the top one toward the “top”.

Longest loop: ______________________　Pushed toward the top, the loop: ______________________

Think: why do parallels get shorter toward the poles? What happens at a pole?

4. Video 2 · Lines from pole to pole (meridians and longitude)

Key points
  • Meridians are half-circles joining the poles; all are the same length and point north–south.
  • In 1884 the meridian through the Royal Observatory, Greenwich, London, was set as 0°, the prime meridian; east up to 180° is east longitude (E), west up to 180° is west longitude (W).
  • Two numbers, latitude + longitude, pin down a place: Jakarta is about 6°S, 107°E. Nearly 2,000 years ago, Ptolemy in Alexandria already listed thousands of places by latitude and longitude.

5. Video 3 · Some special lines

Key points
  • The tropics are at about 23.5°: the point where the noon sun is overhead moves back and forth between them. Syene (Aswan) is near the Tropic of Cancer. Jakarta lies between them and has the sun overhead twice a year.
  • The polar circles are at about 66.5°: inside them there is polar day and polar night.
  • The equator divides the Northern and Southern Hemispheres; the circle of 20°W and 160°E divides the Eastern and Western (without cutting countries in Europe and Africa).

6. Think and write

a. Retell: using movie-theater seats, explain to someone who hasn’t taken this lesson how the grid helps us say where a place is.

Sample: a theater uses two numbers, row and seat; the Earth uses latitude and longitude. Parallels count from 0° at the equator to 90° N and S; meridians count from 0° at the prime meridian to 180° E and W; where two lines cross is the place.

b. Evaluate: a classmate says, “The prime meridian divides the Eastern and Western Hemispheres.” Is he right? Name the two meridians usually used, and why.

Sample: not quite. We usually use the circle of 20°W and 160°E: the prime meridian would cut through Britain, France and some West African countries, while 20°W and 160°E run mostly over the ocean.
